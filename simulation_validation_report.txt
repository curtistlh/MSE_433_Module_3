Simulation Validation Report — Methodology
===========================================

1. Our Approach to the Conveyor Problem

The conveyor system we worked with has one input conveyor that feeds into a loop of four picking conveyors. Totes containing items are loaded onto the input conveyor one at a time. Each item travels through the loop, and when it reaches a picking conveyor whose active order needs that item type, the scanner diverts it off the belt. If the item is not needed by any active order on that conveyor, it continues around the loop and comes back again. Each conveyor handles exactly one order at a time, and an order cannot be split across multiple conveyors, but all four conveyors can work on their respective orders simultaneously.

Given this setup, there are three main decisions that determine performance: (1) which conveyor each order is assigned to, (2) in what sequence the totes are released onto the input conveyor, and (3) how items within each tote are ordered for release. The goal is to minimize makespan, which is the total elapsed time from the first item entering the system to the last item being picked. A poor assignment or a bad tote sequence can leave one conveyor overloaded while the other three sit idle, or it can flood the loop with items that no active order currently needs, blocking the useful items from getting through.


2. The Four Heuristics

We tested four heuristics against each other:

FIFO (First In, First Out) processes everything in the order it arrives. Orders are assigned to conveyors by simple round-robin, and totes are released in the order they first appear in the data. There is no intelligence and no look-ahead. It is the simplest possible approach.

Assignment First starts by deciding which conveyor each order goes to, using a load-balancing rule that assigns the largest unassigned order to the least-loaded conveyor. Once the conveyor assignments are fixed, it sequences totes by picking the tote that helps the most active orders, and within each tote it releases items for active orders before items for later orders. The key idea is to make the high-level assignment decision well and then work within that structure.

Greedy sorts orders by size (smallest first, known as Shortest Processing Time or SPT) and assigns them to the least-loaded conveyor. For tote sequencing, it scores each available tote by how many items it can contribute to the currently active orders, weighted by how close each order is to the front of its conveyor queue. It always picks the highest-scoring tote next. The idea is to make the locally best choice at every step.

Order Driven sorts orders by remaining work (Shortest Remaining Processing Time or SRPT) and focuses on completing orders one at a time. For each active order, it finds the tote with the best overlap with what that order still needs, pulls it next, and repeats until the order is done before moving on. Orders are assigned to conveyors by round-robin based on the SRPT sequence.


3. Why We Chose Each Heuristic

We chose FIFO as our baseline because it is the most commonly encountered scheduling rule across our Management Engineering courses. We have seen it in inventory management, facility management, scheduling, statistics, and operations research since first year, and it appears in everyday life in every queue from coffee shops to print jobs. It requires zero decision-making intelligence, which makes it the natural control group: any heuristic that beats FIFO is demonstrably adding value through its logic.

We chose the Greedy heuristic because greedy algorithms are one of the most fundamental problem-solving strategies from our algorithms, data structures, and machine learning courses. In those courses, we learned that making the locally optimal choice at each step often produces a good solution quickly, even if it is not globally optimal. For this problem, the greedy approach combines SPT ordering (which is provably optimal for minimizing total completion time on a single machine) with load balancing and urgency-based tote selection, giving it a strong theoretical foundation while still running in milliseconds.

We chose Order Driven because it comes directly from the scheduling course we are currently taking, where we study priority rules like SRPT for job-shop and flow-shop problems. The idea of focusing on completing the order closest to being done is a well-known warehouse practice: it reduces work-in-progress and keeps conveyors from sitting idle waiting for items that are scattered across many totes. It is a natural fit for a system where each conveyor handles one order at a time.

We chose Assignment First because it comes from our operations research and facility planning courses, where we learned about hierarchical decision-making and problem decomposition. In those courses, we saw that when a problem is too complex to solve all at once, fixing some decisions first (the strategic assignment) and then optimizing the rest (the tactical sequencing) often works very well. This approach also reflects how real warehouse systems operate: lane assignments are typically decided upfront based on workload distribution, and detailed sequencing is handled within that framework.


4. Our Expectations Before Testing

Before running any experiments, we expected FIFO to be the most stable but probably the slowest, since it makes no effort to optimize anything. We expected Greedy to sometimes be fast because SPT tends to minimize completion time, but we also expected it to be risky because greedy decisions can create imbalances when multiple conveyors share one input bottleneck. We expected Order Driven to do well because finishing active orders quickly should free up conveyors faster and reduce congestion. We expected Assignment First to be strong because load balancing across conveyors should prevent the single-conveyor bottleneck that dominates makespan in a parallel system. These expectations were grounded in the same course material and practical intuition that motivated each heuristic choice.


5. What We Did: Physical Testing and Simulation

We tested the heuristics in two complementary ways. First, we ran all four on the physical Ideas Clinic conveyor system using the same first 6 orders. Each heuristic produced a CSV input file that was fed into the conveyor, and the system recorded timestamps for every item pick. In that one-time physical test, Assignment First was the fastest at 112.2 seconds, followed by FIFO at 124.9 seconds, Order Driven at 132.2 seconds, and Greedy at 136.1 seconds.

However, a single physical run per heuristic is not enough to draw strong conclusions. Human tote-loading speed, sensor timing variability, and run-to-run setup differences all introduce noise. So we built a shared simulation model to validate the comparison on many more order sets. We verified the simulation against the physical results at the conveyor level: Assignment First matched the physical pick sequence exactly on all 4 conveyors, and FIFO, Greedy, and Order Driven each matched exactly on 3 out of 4 conveyors. The pick counts matched perfectly for all four heuristics. The physical system was consistently 3.6x to 5.8x slower than the simulation in absolute time, which is expected because the simulation does not include manual loading delays, human hesitation, or mechanical variability. The important point is that the simulation reproduces the correct item pick logic and ordering, not that the absolute times match.


6. Simulation Design

The simulation was designed to mirror the real conveyor system as closely as possible while remaining simple enough to run thousands of times. It models one input conveyor with a two-slot pipe feeding into a four-conveyor picking loop. Each picking conveyor has two slots (entry and exit). At each one-second time step, the simulator performs five operations in order: (1) inter-belt transfers, where items move from the end of one conveyor to the start of the next in the loop, with items exiting conveyor 4 recirculating back to conveyor 1; (2) input pipe transfer into conveyor 1; (3) diversion logic at each conveyor midpoint, where the scanner checks if the item matches what the active order needs and picks it if so; (4) internal movement within each conveyor from entry to exit slot; and (5) controlled induction, where the next item from the release queue enters the input pipe if there is space. The conveyor traversal time is 2 seconds, the release slot spacing is 1 second, and the loop capacity is capped at 7 items to prevent flooding.

We used the same random data generator logic as the provided MSE433 notebook to create new order instances. The only practical adjustment was capping item types to the 8 shapes supported by the physical system, because the generator can occasionally produce a ninth item type that has no physical counterpart. Each generated instance produces 10 to 15 orders with 1 to 3 item types per order, drawn from 15 to 20 totes.


7. The 50-Run Validation Study

For the main validation result, we ran exactly 50 generated instances using seeds 100 through 149. We chose a fixed set of 50 runs because it is the most honest and academically defensible design. A fixed sample avoids cherry-picking only the easy instances, applies the same test set to every heuristic, and gives a large enough sample to see meaningful patterns. Each of the 50 seeds generated a different random order set, and all four heuristics were run on the exact same order set for each seed so the comparison is fair.

The results across all 50 runs, treating failed runs with a penalty makespan, were:

  Assignment First: 18/50 successful, mean makespan 55.7s on successful runs, 11 wins
  FIFO:             16/50 successful, mean makespan 57.3s on successful runs, 12 wins
  Order Driven:     14/50 successful, mean makespan 61.9s on successful runs, 8 wins
  Greedy:            6/50 successful, mean makespan 59.5s on successful runs, 1 win

Assignment First had the highest success rate (36%), the best successful-run mean makespan (55.7 seconds), and the most balanced conveyor usage. FIFO was a close second in success rate (32%) and actually won the most individual seeds (12 wins to Assignment First's 11), but its successful-run average was slightly slower. Order Driven succeeded on 14 out of 50 runs and was competitive when it finished, but it was less reliable overall. Greedy was the least reliable of the four, succeeding on only 6 out of 50 runs.

Out of the 50 generated instances, only 3 seeds (121, 125, and 128) allowed all four heuristics to finish successfully. This is a strikingly low number, and it requires careful explanation.


8. Why Only 3 Out of 50 Seeds Had All Four Heuristics Succeed

The low joint-success rate is not caused by a bug in the code or by the simulator simply needing more runtime. We investigated every failed run in detail and confirmed that 100% of the failures entered a repeated deterministic state, meaning the simulator reached a point where the exact same configuration of items on belts, items in the queue, and active order requirements repeated itself. Once the state repeats, it will repeat forever, so no amount of additional simulation time would have helped.

The root cause is a deadlock-like conveyor flow problem that arises from the interaction between three system constraints:

First, the single input conveyor is a serial bottleneck. Every item in the system must enter through the same input pipe, which means items are released strictly one at a time. If the next item in the release queue is not useful to any active order, it still enters the system and takes up space on the loop.

Second, each conveyor only picks items for its one active order. The scanner at each conveyor midpoint checks whether the item matches what the current active order needs. If the item does not match, it passes through and continues around the loop. This means items that were released for orders that have not started yet, or for orders on other conveyors that have already been satisfied for that item type, will just keep circulating indefinitely.

Third, the release queue is ordered by the heuristic's tote sequence, and the simulator does not reorder it. If the heuristic chose a tote sequence that front-loads items for orders that are deep in the conveyor queue, those items enter the loop early but cannot be picked because the conveyor is still working on an earlier order. They fill up the loop, and the items that the active orders actually need stay trapped further back in the release queue.

The combination of these three factors creates the deadlock: unhelpful items circulate endlessly on the loop, the loop fills up, induction slows or stops, and the items that active orders still need never get released. The median time at which the deadlock state first appeared was only about 30 seconds into the simulation, which means the mismatch between release order and active demand shows up very early.

This is not specific to one heuristic. All four heuristics are vulnerable to it, but to different degrees. Greedy was the most vulnerable (44 out of 50 runs failed) because its SPT ordering and urgency-based tote selection can aggressively front-load items for small orders on one conveyor while starving the others. FIFO and Order Driven had moderate vulnerability (34 and 36 failures respectively). Assignment First was the least vulnerable (32 failures) because its load-balancing conveyor assignment distributes demand more evenly, which reduces the chance that one conveyor's active order needs items that are stuck behind another conveyor's items in the queue.

It is also important to understand that this deadlock behavior reflects a real property of the physical system, not just a simulation artifact. In the real conveyor, if you release totes in the wrong order, items will physically recirculate and the same bottleneck occurs. The physical system does not have this problem as severely in practice because operators can adapt in real time (e.g., skipping a tote, reordering items manually), but the underlying structural risk is the same. The simulation enforces the rules strictly, which exposes worst-case behavior that the physical system masks through human flexibility.


9. Why This Is Still a Valid and Sufficient Result

Despite only 3 out of 50 seeds having all four heuristics succeed jointly, the 50-run study still gives us a strong and defensible conclusion. Here is why.

First, we are not comparing heuristics only on the 3 jointly successful seeds. Each heuristic had enough individual successful runs to compute meaningful statistics: Assignment First succeeded 18 times, FIFO succeeded 16 times, Order Driven succeeded 14 times, and Greedy succeeded 6 times. These are independent, fair samples drawn from the same 50 generated instances, and they are large enough to see clear performance differences.

Second, the success rate itself is an important performance metric, not just noise to be filtered out. A heuristic that succeeds on more instances is genuinely better for this system, because it means its sequencing and assignment decisions are more robust to different order configurations. Assignment First's 36% success rate versus Greedy's 12% is a meaningful and practically relevant difference. In a real warehouse, you want the method that works reliably across different order mixes, not just the one that is fastest on the rare easy cases.

Third, the results are internally consistent across both the physical test and the simulation. In the physical experiment, Assignment First was the fastest. In the simulation, Assignment First had the highest success rate, the best mean makespan on successful runs (55.7 seconds), and the best penalty-adjusted mean makespan. The physical pick sequences matched the simulation closely (4/4 exact conveyor matches for Assignment First). This convergence between two independent evaluation methods, one physical and one simulated, strengthens the conclusion considerably.

Fourth, we deliberately chose a fixed 50-run design instead of searching for only the easy seeds. Reporting only the 3 jointly successful seeds would give a tiny sample that hides the system's real difficulty. Reporting the full 50 runs is more honest because it shows both how the heuristics perform when they work and how often they fail. This is the academically safer and more complete result.

In summary, Assignment First is the strongest heuristic for this conveyor problem. It won the physical experiment, it had the highest simulation success rate, it had the best average makespan among successful runs, and its simulation behavior matched the physical system most closely. FIFO is a solid and surprisingly competitive baseline, which confirms the well-known operations research finding that simple rules can perform well when complex heuristics are not designed for the specific system bottleneck. Order Driven showed useful scheduling logic but was less reliable than Assignment First or FIFO on random instances. Greedy, despite its strong theoretical foundation in SPT, was the least suited to this particular system because its aggressive local optimization created the most severe release-order mismatches under the single-input, recirculating conveyor constraints.
