Heuristic Selection Rationale
=============================

1) FIFO Baseline — Why We Chose It
-----------------------------------
We chose First In, First Out as our baseline because it is the simplest and most 
commonly encountered scheduling rule in both academic and real-world settings. We 
have seen FIFO across nearly every management engineering course since first year — 
inventory management, facility management, scheduling, statistics, and operations 
research. It shows up in everyday life too: lineups at a coffee shop, print queues, 
traffic flow, and warehouse order processing all follow FIFO naturally.

FIFO makes sense as a baseline specifically because it requires zero intelligence. 
There is no optimization, no look-ahead, and no prioritization. Items are processed 
in the exact order they arrive, which means any heuristic that performs better than 
FIFO is demonstrably adding value through its decision-making logic. In experimental 
design terms, FIFO is the control group — it tells us what happens when we make no 
effort to optimize, so we can measure how much improvement each heuristic actually 
brings.


2) Greedy Heuristic — Why We Chose It
--------------------------------------
The greedy approach comes directly from our algorithms and data structures courses, 
as well as our machine learning coursework. Greedy algorithms are one of the most 
fundamental problem-solving strategies in computer science: at each step, make the 
locally optimal choice and move on. We have used greedy methods in data cleaning, 
feature selection, graph traversal, and countless other problems throughout our 
academic and work experience.

We chose a greedy heuristic because it strikes a practical balance between solution 
quality and computational cost. Unlike FIFO, it actually thinks about what it is 
doing — it sorts orders by size (shortest first), balances load across conveyors, 
and picks totes based on urgency. But unlike the MILP, it runs in milliseconds and 
does not require a solver. In real warehouse settings, decisions often need to be 
made quickly, so a fast heuristic that produces good (not necessarily optimal) 
solutions is extremely valuable. The greedy approach also has a well-known 
theoretical foundation: Shortest Processing Time (SPT) ordering is provably optimal 
for minimizing total completion time on a single machine, and it remains a strong 
heuristic for parallel machine scheduling.


3) Order Driven Heuristic — Why We Chose It
--------------------------------------------
The order driven heuristic comes from our scheduling course, where we study 
job-shop and flow-shop sequencing problems. The core idea is Shortest Remaining 
Processing Time (SRPT): always work on the order that is closest to being finished. 
This is a well-known priority rule in production scheduling and warehouse operations.

We chose this approach because it directly reflects how many real warehouses 
actually operate — they focus on completing one order at a time before moving to the 
next. The heuristic pairs SRPT ordering with a best-match tote selection strategy, 
where it picks the tote that covers the most remaining demand for the active order. 
This mirrors the practical warehouse logic of "finish what you started before 
grabbing new work," which reduces work-in-progress and order lead times. It is a 
natural fit for our conveyor system because each conveyor processes one order at a 
time anyway.


4) Assignment First Heuristic — Why We Chose It
------------------------------------------------
The Assignment First approach comes from our operations research and facility 
planning courses, where we learned about hierarchical decision-making and problem 
decomposition. The idea is to break a complex joint decision into smaller sequential 
sub-decisions: first decide which conveyor each order goes to (the assignment), then 
figure out the tote sequencing and item ordering around that fixed assignment.

We chose this because it reflects how real warehouse systems often work in practice. 
Conveyor lane assignments are typically decided upfront based on zone allocation, 
workload distribution, or physical layout constraints — they are a strategic 
decision. The detailed sequencing of totes and items is then a tactical decision 
made within that fixed framework. By separating these two levels of planning, the 
problem becomes much easier to reason about and solve. This decomposition principle 
is something we have encountered repeatedly in our optimization and supply chain 
courses: when a problem is too complex to solve all at once, fix some decisions 
first and optimize the rest. The Assignment First heuristic tests whether making 
the conveyor assignment decision well (through load balancing) and then focusing on 
completing active orders gives us good overall performance.


5) MILP — Why We Chose It
--------------------------
Mixed Integer Linear Programming is one of the core techniques from our optimization 
courses. Unlike the heuristics above, the MILP formulates the entire problem 
mathematically — every decision variable, constraint, and objective — and hands it 
to a solver to find the provably best (or near-best) solution. We included it 
because it serves as our upper bound on solution quality: it tells us how close the 
heuristics are getting to the true optimum.

We chose MILP because it is the gold standard for combinatorial optimization in 
operations research. It jointly optimizes order sequencing, conveyor assignment, 
tote release order, and recirculation timing — all the decisions that the heuristics 
handle separately or approximately. The tradeoff is computation time: the MILP can 
take 40-60 minutes to solve, which is impractical for real-time warehouse decisions 
but invaluable for benchmarking. By comparing each heuristic's performance against 
the MILP solution, we can quantify the gap between fast approximate methods and the 
theoretical optimum.


================================================================================
Physical Experiment Results — Comparison and Analysis
================================================================================

We ran all five methods on the physical Ideas Clinic conveyor system using the 
first 6 orders. Each heuristic produced an input CSV that was fed into the conveyor, 
and the system recorded timestamps for every item pick. The physical output files 
are named grp_2_<method>_output.csv.

All methods processed the same 20 items (6 orders) across 4 conveyors with the 
same item distribution per conveyor: 7 items on Conv 1, 7 on Conv 2, 2 on Conv 3, 
and 4 on Conv 4.


Results Summary
---------------
                        Total Makespan    Conv 1 Done    Conv 2 Done    Conv 3 Done    Conv 4 Done
Assignment First:          112.2s           81.6s         112.2s          65.3s          92.8s
FIFO Baseline:             124.9s           85.2s         106.9s          75.4s         124.9s
Order Driven:              132.2s           45.0s         125.8s         110.9s         132.2s
Greedy Heuristic:          136.1s           39.4s         136.1s          82.9s         107.2s

Ranking by total makespan (fastest to slowest):
  1. Assignment First    — 112.2s
  2. FIFO Baseline       — 124.9s  (+11.3% slower)
  3. Order Driven        — 132.2s  (+17.8% slower)
  4. Greedy Heuristic    — 136.1s  (+21.3% slower)


Analysis: Why Assignment First Won
-----------------------------------
Assignment First achieved the best makespan primarily because of load balance. 
Its per-conveyor completion times are the most evenly spread: the gap between its 
fastest and slowest conveyor is only 46.9 seconds (65.3s to 112.2s). No single 
conveyor became a severe bottleneck.

In contrast, the Greedy Heuristic finished Conv 1 extremely fast (39.4s) but left 
Conv 2 running until 136.1s — a spread of 96.7 seconds. This means Conv 1 sat idle 
for almost 100 seconds while Conv 2 was still working. The Greedy approach optimized 
too aggressively for finishing early orders quickly (SPT), which front-loaded work 
onto Conv 1 and starved Conv 2 of early totes. In a system with a single input 
conveyor bottleneck, this imbalance is costly.

Assignment First's strategy of deciding conveyor assignments upfront and then 
sequencing totes to complete the active order on each conveyor produced a more 
balanced workload across all four conveyors, which is ultimately what determines 
makespan in a parallel system.


Analysis: Why FIFO Beat the Greedy and Order Driven Heuristics
--------------------------------------------------------------
This is a surprising result — the "dumb" baseline outperformed two intelligent 
heuristics. Several factors explain this:

1. Single input conveyor bottleneck. All items enter through one input conveyor 
   before reaching their assigned picking conveyor. The heuristics that 
   aggressively prioritize certain orders (Greedy focuses on small orders first, 
   Order Driven focuses on closest-to-completion) end up creating clusters of 
   items for one or two conveyors while leaving the others idle. FIFO distributes 
   items more naturally because it does not try to cluster anything.

2. Recirculation penalty. When items arrive at a conveyor that is still busy with 
   a previous order, they recirculate through the full loop (1 -> 2 -> 3 -> 4 -> 1) 
   and waste time. The Greedy and Order Driven heuristics sometimes released totes 
   that contained items for orders assigned to busy conveyors, forcing those items 
   to recirculate multiple times. FIFO's naive ordering happened to avoid the worst 
   cases of this by spreading tote releases more evenly.

3. Greedy's SPT bias created an imbalanced schedule. By putting the smallest orders 
   first, the Greedy heuristic finished Conv 1 and Conv 3 very quickly but pushed 
   the larger orders (Order 2 and Order 5) entirely to Conv 2. Conv 2 ended up 
   processing 7 items sequentially with some items arriving late because their 
   totes were released after totes for the already-finished conveyors. The result 
   was a long tail on Conv 2 (136.1s) that dominated the makespan.

4. Order Driven had similar clustering issues. By always working on the 
   closest-to-completion order, the Order Driven heuristic pulled totes out of 
   sequence to satisfy immediate demand, which delayed tote releases for orders 
   on other conveyors. Conv 4 did not receive its first item until 95.5 seconds 
   into the run, meaning it sat idle for over a minute and a half.


The Role of Human Error
------------------------
It is important to note that these are physical experiments, not simulations. 
Several sources of variability and human error could have affected the results:

1. Tote loading speed. The operator manually loads totes onto the input conveyor. 
   Variations in how quickly totes were loaded (fumbling, hesitation, reading the 
   sequence list) directly affect when items enter the system. A 2-3 second delay 
   on a tote propagates to every item in that tote.

2. Physical pick timing. The conveyor system's sensors and mechanical diverters 
   have inherent variability. The same item on two different runs might be picked 
   0.5-2 seconds apart due to sensor response time, belt speed fluctuation, or 
   item positioning on the belt.

3. Tote preparation. If a tote was not loaded with the correct items or in the 
   right position before the experiment, time could be lost re-sorting or 
   correcting mid-run.

4. Run-to-run variability. Each heuristic was tested in a separate physical run. 
   Differences in operator readiness, system warm-up, or minor mechanical issues 
   between runs could account for several seconds of difference. This means that 
   small performance gaps (under ~5 seconds) may not be statistically significant.

5. Learning effect. If the heuristics were tested in a fixed order, the operator 
   may have gotten faster at loading totes in later runs. This could slightly 
   advantage whichever method was tested last.

Given these sources of error, the 12.7-second gap between Assignment First (112.2s) 
and FIFO (124.9s) is likely meaningful. The 11.2-second gap between FIFO and 
Order Driven (132.2s) is also probably real. However, the 3.9-second gap between 
Order Driven and Greedy (136.1s) is within the range where human error could be 
a factor.


Key Takeaways
--------------
1. Load balance matters more than local optimality. In a parallel conveyor system 
   with a shared input conveyor, the method that distributes work most evenly 
   across conveyors wins. Assignment First achieved this by making the conveyor 
   assignment decision upfront and then working to complete each conveyor's active 
   order as quickly as possible.

2. Aggressive optimization can backfire. The Greedy heuristic's SPT strategy is 
   theoretically strong for single-machine scheduling, but on a parallel system 
   with a shared input bottleneck, it created severe imbalances. Finishing one 
   conveyor in 39 seconds is meaningless if another takes 136 seconds.

3. FIFO is a surprisingly tough baseline. Its lack of intelligence turned out to 
   be an advantage in one sense: it did not create the artificial clustering and 
   imbalance that the smarter heuristics did. This is a well-known phenomenon in 
   operations research — simple rules can outperform complex ones when the complex 
   ones are not designed for the specific system constraints.

4. The single input conveyor is the dominant constraint. All items must pass 
   through one entry point, which means tote release order is the single most 
   impactful decision. Heuristics that do not account for this bottleneck when 
   making sequencing decisions will underperform.
